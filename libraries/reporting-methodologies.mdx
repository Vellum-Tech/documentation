A **reporting methodology** is the kind of report you're writing. Not a formula, not a calculation, but a protocol. It sets what your report is arguing, who it's arguing to, what evidence you'll be expected to show, and what a reader should take away.

You choose it for a project *before* you start valuing outcomes, because it shapes everything that follows. An SROI report and a Cost-Benefit Analysis of the same programme ask for different evidence, present different outputs, and are read by different people.

## Why it exists

Two reasons.

The first is that "what sort of report is this?" is a real decision with real consequences, and it deserves to be recorded rather than assumed. Choosing **Cost-Benefit Analysis (CBA)** commits you to a discount rate, a counterfactual and a sensitivity analysis. Choosing **SROI / sSROI** commits you to stakeholders, financial proxies and adjustments for attribution, deadweight and drop-off. Making that choice explicit and early means nobody discovers halfway through that they've been building the wrong evidence base.

The second is comparability. When a reader knows which protocol a report follows, they know how to weigh it, and they know what its limitations are likely to be, without having to reverse-engineer them from your appendices.

## What's in the library

Six protocols. They ship with Vellum, they're the same for every firm, and they don't change, so this library is read-only. There's no create button and no edit. The six are the six.

<Note>
  This layer carries **no monetary value of its own**. A reporting methodology sets the pathway. The actual valuing is done by the [methodologies](/libraries/methodologies) in your formula catalogue, and every figure is computed by the engine.
</Note>

## The list

Open **Libraries > Reporting methodology library**. The list shows each protocol's **Methodology** name, its **Primary purpose**, its **Best fit** audience and its **Typical output**, which is enough to choose between them at a glance. Search matches all four. **View options** lets you change the ordering and hide columns you don't need.

Click any row for the full record.

## The record

Each record has three cards.

**Methodology** carries the four guidance columns: **Primary purpose** (what the methodology shows), **Best-fit audience** (who it suits), **Evidence required** (what it demands of you) and **Typical output** (what it produces).

**Bridge to valuation methods** explains how this protocol connects down to the outcome-level valuation methods that do the arithmetic, and which sorts of methodology it typically draws on. It also carries the reminder that a reporting methodology sets the pathway and nothing more. The figures live in the engine.

**Reader interpretation** is the one to read before you promise a client anything. It says how a reader should weigh the report and where this protocol is weak.

## The six protocols

### SROI / sSROI

**What it shows:** social value created relative to the resources invested.

**Best fit:** charities, foundations, community programmes, social enterprises and philanthropy portfolios.

**Evidence required:** stakeholders, inputs, outcomes, financial proxies, attribution, deadweight, displacement, duration and drop-off.

**Typical output:** social value, an SROI ratio, stakeholder outcomes, sensitivity analysis and narrative.

**Links to valuation methods:** uses methods such as avoided cost, replacement cost, wellbeing proxy and direct-cost fallback for individual outcomes.

**How a reader should weigh it:** good for understanding value-for-money and stakeholder benefit, but it relies heavily on proxy quality and attribution assumptions.

### Cost-Benefit Analysis (CBA)

**What it shows:** quantified benefits compared against costs, usually over time.

**Best fit:** government policy, infrastructure, hospitals, schools, transport, affordable living, grants and public programmes.

**Evidence required:** project costs, benefit streams, scenarios, a discount rate, timing, a counterfactual and sensitivity analysis.

**Typical output:** net present value, benefit-cost ratio, distributional analysis and scenario comparison.

**Links to valuation methods:** uses methods such as avoided public cost, productivity uplift, willingness-to-pay, carbon price and health cost savings.

**How a reader should weigh it:** strong for policy and investment decisions where readers need scenario discipline and public-value comparability.

### Impact Weighted Accounting

**What it shows:** environmental and social outcomes translated into monetary impact that can sit alongside financial reporting.

**Best fit:** corporates, investors, asset managers, large accounting firms, ratings platforms and annual-report readers.

**Evidence required:** standardised outcome units, a defined entity boundary, attribution, unit values, confidence, time period and an audit trail.

**Typical output:** an impact revenue line, an impact-weighted profit and loss, a portfolio impact view and comparable disclosures.

**Links to valuation methods:** uses outcome-level valuation methods, then normalises them for accounting-style presentation.

**How a reader should weigh it:** useful when readers want impact beside financial performance, but the governance of the methodology has to be very clear.

### Choice Modelling / Stated Preference

**What it shows:** value estimated from the choices people make between trade-offs.

**Best fit:** government, infrastructure, health, environment, education, community preference and service design reports.

**Evidence required:** survey design, respondent segments, choice sets, willingness-to-pay estimates, bias controls and statistical review.

**Typical output:** preference values, willingness-to-pay values and scenario comparison.

**Links to valuation methods:** can create financial proxies that later feed SROI, CBA or Impact Weighted Accounting.

**How a reader should weigh it:** useful where no market price exists, but it needs careful survey design and clearly stated limitations.

### Public Value / Policy Evaluation

**What it shows:** outcomes connected to government priorities, public budgets, regulation, grants and community trust.

**Best fit:** government departments, grant programmes, public infrastructure, bonds and public-sector partners.

**Evidence required:** policy objectives, programme logic, beneficiaries, public costs and savings, distributional outcomes, and the political and regulatory context.

**Typical output:** a policy impact dashboard, a programme-level report, acquittal evidence and an improvement plan.

**Links to valuation methods:** may combine CBA, avoided public cost, fiscal return, wellbeing valuation and SDG mapping.

**How a reader should weigh it:** readers can see which initiatives create public value, and where the evidence is still weak.

### Natural Capital / Environmental Accounting

**What it shows:** value created or protected through environmental assets and ecosystem services.

**Best fit:** climate, biodiversity, circular economy, agriculture, land, water, energy and nature-positive projects.

**Evidence required:** environmental units, a baseline, the ecosystem service in question, location, permanence, leakage, carbon and biodiversity factors, and assurance.

**Typical output:** a natural capital account, environmental return, ecosystem service value and an SDG or environmental dashboard.

**Links to valuation methods:** uses carbon price, ecosystem-service proxy, avoided disposal, replacement or restoration cost, and market values.

**How a reader should weigh it:** readers can find climate and nature activities by SDG and compare the evidence behind environmental claims.

## Choosing one

Start with the audience, not the method. A grant acquittal for a state department and an annual report for institutional investors want different things from the same programme, and the protocol that fits is usually obvious once you've named who's reading.

Then check the **Evidence required** line honestly. If a protocol demands a counterfactual and a discount rate and you have neither, that's not a detail to sort out later. It's a reason to choose differently, or to go and get the evidence first.

Finally, read **Reader interpretation** before you commit. Every one of the six has a known weakness. Knowing it upfront lets you address it in the report rather than have a reviewer find it for you.

<Tip>
  You set the reporting methodology on the project itself, not here. This library is the reference you consult while deciding. A project can also use a bespoke methodology that isn't one of the six. The six are what Vellum knows about and can give you guidance for.
</Tip>

## Tips and gotchas

**Choose before you value, not after.** The protocol determines what evidence you need. Discovering it once the impact model is built means rework.

**No numbers live at this level.** If you're looking for a figure, you want the [Methodology library](/libraries/methodologies) and the Workbench. This layer sets the pathway.

**Don't confuse the two libraries.** "Methodology" in Vellum means a valuation formula. "Reporting methodology" means one of these six protocols. They sit at different altitudes and do different jobs.

**The six are fixed.** Nothing here is editable, and that's intentional. These are recognised external protocols, not house style.
